Legislative Update

PA Skill Games: 85 Days Left, One Path Forward — What Operators Need to Know About the Standalone Bill Before October 13

By Skill Games Pennsylvania • July 20, 2026 • 9 min read

Governor Shapiro signed Pennsylvania's $50.8 billion budget on July 13, 2026 — and skill games weren't in it. After months of negotiations and competing tax proposals ranging from 16% to 52%, the final budget deal excluded any framework for regulating the roughly 70,000 machines currently operating across the state. That closes the budget-rider path for good. What remains is a single, narrow window: a standalone bill passed and signed before October 13, 2026, the date the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's 120-day enforcement stay expires.

For PA skill game operators, this is the moment the stakes become completely concrete. A missed deadline doesn't produce another year of legal gray zone — it produces law enforcement with authority to seize machines. Here's where things actually stand as of today.

Why the Budget Omission Matters More Than It Looks

Budgets are the most reliable vehicle for policy riders in Harrisburg because they must pass. Skill games regulation was widely expected to be attached to the 2026-27 budget precisely because the Independent Fiscal Office estimated it could generate more than $1 billion annually for the commonwealth — revenue the state desperately needs given a multi-billion-dollar structural deficit. Senate President Pro Tempore Kim Ward and Majority Leader Joe Pittman had both framed gaming reform as a “critical piece” of the budget negotiations.

It didn't happen. The $50.8B budget deal that eventually emerged prioritized new education funding and resolved a data center tax dispute, but the skill games tax rate gap — the chasm between Shapiro's 52% demand and the Senate Republican caucus's sub-35% position — was too wide to bridge in the final days of negotiations. Shapiro signed the budget and indicated he remains open to continued talks, saying publicly that “my door's open.” But the budget window has closed.

What this means operationally: there is no longer a natural vehicle carrying skill games regulation forward. A standalone bill requires its own committee hearings, floor scheduling, bicameral agreement, and a governor's signature. In a normal legislative calendar, that process takes months. The legislature has roughly 85 days until October 13.

The Legislative Calendar Math

The Pennsylvania General Assembly does not hold floor votes through most of July and August. Lawmakers return for fall session in September, with the House scheduled for a voting session as early as September 9, 2026. That leaves the real working window at approximately five to seven weeks between the legislature's return and the October 13 deadline.

For a standalone bill to make it in time, it would need to:

  • Clear committee in either the House or Senate (or both chambers simultaneously if identical companion bills are run)
  • Pass a floor vote in one chamber
  • Pass a floor vote in the other chamber
  • Reach Shapiro's desk with enough time for him to sign it before October 13

It's not an impossible sequence. Pennsylvania has moved legislation quickly when leadership in both chambers is aligned and a deadline is hard. But it requires a level of political will and pre-negotiated agreement that hasn't yet been publicly demonstrated. The tax rate remains the central unresolved dispute.

The Four Proposals Still in Play

Four distinct tax frameworks have been floated this session, and none has yet secured enough bicameral support to cross the finish line:

Shapiro's 52% — Casino-Parity Rate

Governor Shapiro has consistently pushed for taxing skill games at the same rate applied to Pennsylvania casino slot machines: 52% of gross terminal revenue (GTR). His administration projects this generates approximately $2 billion in revenue over five years. The casino lobby strongly supports this rate, as it would eliminate the competitive price advantage skill games currently enjoy over licensed casino floors. At 52%, a terminal generating $58,000 annually in GTR would carry roughly $30,000 in annual tax liability — a burden that would make many lower-traffic host locations economically unviable.

Senator Gebhard's 35–36% — Middle-Ground Proposal

Senator Chris Gebhard has circulated a proposal in the 35–36% range, positioning it as a compromise that generates meaningful state revenue without the market-killing effect of casino parity. This is closer to the Senate Republican caucus's center of gravity. At 35%, a $58,000-GTR machine would produce roughly $20,300 in annual tax — significant, but workable for higher-volume locations.

Senator Yaw's 16% — Industry-Friendly Floor

Senator Gene Yaw has advocated for a 16% GTR tax, the lowest rate in the formal debate. His argument: a punishing rate doesn't just cut margins, it pushes machines underground or across state lines, producing less revenue than a sustainable rate would. At 16%, the annual tax per $58,000-GTR terminal would be approximately $9,300 — easily absorbed within existing revenue-share structures. Yaw's rate is unlikely to win the governor's signature without meaningful concessions elsewhere.

Representative Benninghoff's No-Additional-Tax Approach

Representative Kerry Benninghoff has put forward a bill calling for regulation without an additional tax burden on skill games beyond what venues already pay in business taxes. This is the industry's preferred floor but the furthest from what the governor will sign. Its value in the negotiation is as an anchor for the low end of the range.

Representative Danilo Burgos has also proposed monthly licensing fees combined with a statewide cap on machine numbers, offering a different structure than a percentage-of-revenue tax. This approach could be grafted onto a compromise deal.

What “My Door's Open” Actually Signals

Shapiro's post-budget statement that his door remains open for skill games discussions is not a policy concession — it's political positioning. He has been consistent on two things throughout this fight: skill games should be regulated, and 52% is his opening number. The fact that the budget passed without skill games doesn't mean he's moved off the rate; it means the rate gap wasn't resolved in time for the budget.

For a standalone deal to happen before October 13, one of two things must occur. Either Shapiro accepts a rate below 52% (likely somewhere in the 35–40% range) in exchange for other provisions he values — such as a lower machine cap or stronger consumer protection requirements. Or the Senate and House agree on a bill at or near 52% that doesn't kill the industry in practice, perhaps by phasing the rate in over time or applying it only to new entrants.

Neither outcome is certain. Both are achievable if there's political will to close the gap in the narrow September window.

What Happens If October 13 Arrives Without a Bill

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court's June 15, 2026 ruling was unambiguous: skill games are slot machines under the state Gaming Act, and operating them without a license violates Pennsylvania gambling law. The court stayed enforcement for 120 days specifically to give the legislature a chance to establish a legal framework. It is not an open-ended stay.

If the legislature fails to pass a bill by October 13, the stay expires. State law enforcement — the Pennsylvania State Police, local police departments, and potentially the Pennsylvania Gaming Control Board — would have clear legal authority to act. Governor Shapiro has publicly stated that state law enforcement is prepared to prevent illegal operation of the games if the deadline passes without a legislative fix.

For operators, this is not a theoretical risk. It is a specific, calendared enforcement trigger. Machines without a regulatory framework become seizable property on October 14.

What PA Skill Game Operators Should Do Right Now

The next 85 days are not passive-waiting time. They're the window to position your operation for whatever comes next. Here's the practical action list:

  1. Contact your state legislators before September 9. The legislature is in recess now, but lawmakers are in their districts. Constituent calls and in-person meetings with state senators and representatives happen during recess. September 9 is the first House floor session — reach lawmakers before they return to Harrisburg. The Pennsylvania Skill Games Association coordinates industry outreach; connect with them if you haven't already.
  2. Audit your location portfolio for rate sensitivity. Run the math at three scenarios: 16%, 35%, and 52% GTR tax on each of your locations. Know exactly which locations remain profitable under each rate, and which don't. This analysis is not hypothetical planning — it's the foundation of any contract renegotiation conversations you'll need to have.
  3. Review and renegotiate revenue-share agreements now. If a regulation bill passes with a tax in the 35–52% range, revenue-share splits will need to reflect that new reality. Get ahead of these conversations with your host locations before legislation creates urgency.
  4. Prepare your compliance documentation. Any regulatory framework will require licensing. Start gathering business entity documents, state and local permits, machine serial numbers, and operator agreement records. The licensing window after a bill passes will move fast, and operators who are ready will have an advantage over those scrambling to assemble paperwork.
  5. Have a contingency plan for October 14. This doesn't mean assuming the worst — it means not being caught flat-footed if the legislature misses the deadline. Know which machines are in which locations, have a plan for orderly removal if necessary, and make sure your host locations understand the timeline.
  6. Monitor September floor votes closely. The first House session back is September 9. If skill games legislation is going to move, it will likely be scheduled in the first two weeks of September to allow time for the other chamber to act. Watch the PA General Assembly's published session calendar for committee and floor scheduling announcements.

The Bottom Line

The budget closed without skill games, which was the cleanest path to a deal. What's left is a harder lift: a standalone bill through a legislature returning from recess with roughly five to seven weeks of voting time before an enforcement deadline backed by a Supreme Court ruling. That's not zero probability — Pennsylvania has moved fast when it needed to. But it requires a tax rate compromise that has eluded Harrisburg for the better part of two years.

Operators who are waiting to see what happens will not have enough time to respond after October 13. The operators who come through this strongest will be the ones who already know their numbers at every tax rate scenario, have their compliance documentation ready, and have been actively engaged with their legislators through the summer. That engagement starts now — not in September.
