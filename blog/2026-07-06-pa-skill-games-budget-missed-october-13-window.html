The June 30 Budget Deadline Passed Without a Deal: PA Skill Game Operators Are Now in the 99-Day October 13 Window

Published July 6, 2026  |  Legislative Update

Pennsylvania’s constitutional budget deadline came and went on June 30 without a skill games regulatory framework attached to it. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s 4-2 ruling on June 15 — which found that skill games are illegal slot machines under state law — left a 120-day enforcement stay that now runs until approximately October 13, 2026. Three competing proposals remain active in Harrisburg: Governor Shapiro’s 52% gross revenue tax, Sen. Chris Gebhard’s 36% rate, and the bipartisan SB 1079 flat fee backed by Senators Yaw, Williams, and Burgos. This is where things stand as of July 6 — and what the next 99 days mean for every operator with machines on the floor.

What the Missed Budget Deadline Actually Means for Operators

The fact that June 30 passed without a skill games deal is not a surprise — it was identified as the most likely near-term outcome in our June 29 analysis. Pennsylvania has a long history of missing its constitutional budget deadline. What matters now is not the missed deadline itself, but what it tells us about the trajectory of negotiations and what it means for the 70,000 machines still operating across the commonwealth.

The operative clock has not changed. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s 120-day enforcement stay runs from June 15, putting the enforcement exposure date at approximately October 13, 2026. That date is fixed. It does not move because the budget is late, and it does not move because negotiations are ongoing. Whether Harrisburg is in active budget talks or full impasse, October 13 arrives regardless.

What the missed June 30 deadline does change is the political geometry. The budget deadline was a forcing function that aligned the incentive to resolve skill games with the incentive to resolve the larger fiscal picture. Now that the budget deadline has passed without resolution, skill games negotiations must proceed either as a standalone track or as a component of a delayed budget package. Both paths remain open. Neither is guaranteed.

The single most important thing operators need to understand: Pennsylvania has never before had a Supreme Court ruling declaring skill games illegal while simultaneously having three active legislative proposals to legalize and regulate them. This is not a normal legislative situation. The pressure on lawmakers to act before October 13 is qualitatively different from any prior session, and the consequences of inaction are concrete and specific: enforcement begins the day the stay expires.

The Summer Legislative Calendar: Three Paths to October 13

The Pennsylvania General Assembly does not run a full session calendar through the summer. After the budget deadline, the legislature typically schedules targeted session weeks rather than continuous sittings. Understanding what the summer calendar actually looks like — and what it means for skill games — is essential for operators trying to track when a vote could realistically happen.

Path 1: A Standalone Skill Games Bill Passes in July or August

This is the scenario the industry has been preparing for. If Senate Republicans — who have called skill games regulation a “critical piece” of fiscal resolution — move quickly on a standalone vehicle after the budget impasse, a bill could clear the Senate floor in July. The House would need to concur, and the Governor would need to sign. That is three steps in a compressed summer calendar, but it is achievable if leadership in both chambers treats it as a priority.

  • Senate Finance and Appropriations Committees are the venues to watch for July committee hearings. A bill that clears committee before the August recess has a realistic shot at floor votes in September — with time to spare before October 13.
  • Senator Gebhard’s 36% framework and the SB 1079 flat fee are both positioned to move quickly; both have been debated in committee and have broad co-sponsor lists. A standalone bill based on either framework requires fewer concessions than a budget-linked package and could move faster.
  • The Pennsylvania Tavern Association (PA-TAP) and Pace-O-Matic are actively lobbying for a deal. Skill game supporters have rallied at the Capitol in recent weeks, with state legislators including Rep. Jarrett Coleman and Senators Anthony Williams and Chris Gebhard speaking in support of a framework that keeps the industry viable. PA-TAP’s legislative alerts and Pace-O-Matic’s public communications are the fastest real-time signal for operators watching bill movement.

Path 2: Skill Games Roll Into a Late Budget Package in August or September

Pennsylvania sometimes resolves budget impasses with omnibus packages that include policy riders. If the main budget disagreement is resolved in August or early September, skill games could be included as a revenue attachment — exactly the position they occupied in the Shapiro administration’s original proposal. This path produces a later deal but still hits before the October 13 deadline if the budget resolves by mid-September.

  • This path is more likely to favor Shapiro’s preferred rate. When skill games are embedded in a budget package rather than a standalone bill, the Governor’s negotiating leverage is greater because he controls whether to sign the whole package.
  • Operators should watch the Independent Fiscal Office’s official revenue projections in July. If the IFO publishes an updated estimate tied to a specific rate, it is a signal that the Shapiro administration and legislative negotiators are working off the same numbers — a prerequisite for a budget deal that includes skill games.

Path 3: No Deal Before October 13

This outcome becomes more likely with each week that passes without a framework. Pennsylvania’s legislative history includes extended impasses: the 2019 budget fight ran into November. If budget negotiations consume all the available session bandwidth and skill games are treated as secondary, operators could reach October 13 with no protective framework in place.

  • In this scenario, exposure is not uniform. Machines at PLCB-licensed bars, restaurants, and taverns — operated by documented business entities — present a different enforcement picture than machines at gas stations or unlicensed locations.
  • Pennsylvania gaming attorneys have identified PLCB liquor licensees as a specific group facing compliance challenges: the Supreme Court ruling creates potential license jeopardy for establishments hosting machines that are now legally classified as slot machines. If you hold a PLCB license, this is a direct licensing exposure that warrants a conversation with a gaming or liquor license attorney before September.
  • Machines that are not in PLCB-licensed locations should be evaluated for relocation now, while there is time. Finding a qualified PLCB-licensed host venue typically takes four to eight weeks when done proactively; under an October deadline with other operators competing for the same locations, that timeline compresses significantly.

The Three Active Proposals: Updated July Status

All three major proposals remain alive in Harrisburg. None has advanced to a floor vote. Their positions have not materially shifted since June 30, but the July window changes the political calculus around each one.

One provision likely to appear in any final package regardless of which rate wins: Rep. Ben Waxman’s HB 2557 consumer protection overlay. Introduced June 1, HB 2557 sets a $250 daily loss cap, requires age-21 identity verification, and bans skill games from gas stations and venues without PLCB licenses. House Democrats have made clear that some form of these consumer protections is a condition of any floor vote in the lower chamber. Operators need to understand what HB 2557 means for their specific location types before any framework passes.

The PLCB Angle: Why Liquor License Holders Face a Specific Risk

The Supreme Court’s ruling has created a compliance challenge for Pennsylvania liquor licensees that is distinct from the general enforcement risk facing skill game operators. PLCB regulations prohibit liquor-licensed establishments from operating illegal gambling devices on their premises. Now that the Pennsylvania Supreme Court has declared skill games to be illegal slot machines — even with the 120-day stay suspending enforcement of the gaming statutes — there is a live question about whether the stay protects PLCB licensees from liquor board action as well.

Pennsylvania gaming attorneys have flagged this as an unresolved legal question with real exposure. The PLCB has historically deferred to gaming and criminal enforcement on these issues, but its independent authority to act against a licensee for operating illegal gambling equipment is not suspended by a Supreme Court stay in the gaming context. In plain terms: a bar or tavern with a PLCB license and skill game machines may face two separate regulatory bodies with two separate enforcement calendars, and the Supreme Court’s stay addresses only one of them.

If you operate skill games in a location that also holds a PLCB liquor license, this is the specific question you need answered before September: Does the Supreme Court’s 120-day stay protect you from PLCB enforcement, or only from Pennsylvania State Police and criminal prosecution? That question requires a conversation with a Pennsylvania attorney who understands both gaming law and liquor licensing — and that conversation should happen in July, not October.

99-Day Action Plan: What to Do Before October 13

Weeks 1–2 (Now Through July 20): Complete Your Portfolio Audit

  • Create a complete inventory of every machine you operate: street address, venue name, venue type, PLCB license number (if applicable), machine make and model, and serial number. This is the foundation of any licensing application under any framework.
  • Flag every placement at a gas station, unlicensed convenience store, or any venue that does not hold a current PLCB liquor license. Under all three active proposals, these locations will not qualify for licensing. Begin conversations with those hosts about the regulatory outlook this week.
  • Document your revenue-share agreements with host venues. Knowing your contractual terms now gives you the ability to renegotiate before the licensing window opens rather than after.

Weeks 3–5 (July 20 – August 10): Model Your Financials Under Each Scenario

  • Run the numbers for every machine at all three rate structures. At 52% gross revenue tax on a machine generating $3,000/month, the annual tax liability is approximately $18,720. At 36%, approximately $12,960. At the $500 flat fee, $6,000 annually regardless of revenue. Machines that are marginal at the flat fee are likely unviable at 52%.
  • Identify your break-even revenue per machine under each scenario. Locations running below that threshold under 52% but above it at 36% or the flat fee represent your portfolio’s rate-sensitive exposure. Know which locations those are before a bill passes.
  • If your revenue-share arrangement is a 50/50 split with the host venue, model what renegotiation looks like under each tax scenario. Operators who renegotiate terms before licensing opens will end up with better economics than those who renegotiate after a specific tax rate is law.

Weeks 6–9 (August 10 – September 7): Watch the Legislature and Engage It

  • Monitor the Senate Finance Committee and House Gaming Oversight Committee calendars. A committee hearing in August on any of the three active proposals is the signal that a floor vote before October 13 is realistic. Absence of committee activity through mid-August is a warning sign.
  • Contact your State Senator and State Representative directly. The message is simple and fiscal: regulated skill games at a workable rate generate more than $1 billion annually for Pennsylvania, while an unworkable rate produces machine shutdowns and zero revenue. Your machine count, host venue locations, and the number of employees who depend on the revenue all matter to a legislator who represents that district.
  • PA-TAP and Pace-O-Matic are coordinating operator outreach and have direct relationships with committee chairs and floor leaders. If you are not already receiving their legislative alerts, connect with them before August.

Weeks 10–14 (September 7 – October 13): Final Compliance Preparations

  • If a framework has passed by September 7, the licensing application window will likely be open or imminent. Move immediately: have your machine inventory, venue contracts, business entity documents, and monitoring compatibility confirmations ready. Licensing under a machine cap with first-come allocation means speed of application matters as much as eligibility.
  • Confirm firmware compatibility with your machine distributor. Banilla Games, JVL Entertainment, and Primero are the three primary manufacturers distributed in Pennsylvania. Any licensing framework will require machines to be compatible with the Pennsylvania Gaming Control Board’s monitoring system.
  • If no framework has passed by September 7 and there is no active floor vote scheduled, treat October 13 as a hard stop date. Have a plan for which locations you pull machines from, which attorney you call, and which PLCB-licensed backup locations you have already identified and pre-qualified.
  • Have a Pennsylvania gaming attorney identified and available before the stay expires. The period from October 13 onward — if no framework has passed — requires real-time legal guidance that takes weeks to arrange when done cold.

The Bottom Line

The June 30 budget deadline was a pressure point. The October 13 enforcement deadline is a hard stop. Between now and then, Pennsylvania’s legislature has a realistic window to pass a regulatory framework that keeps the industry alive and generates more than $1 billion annually in state revenue. The political conditions for a deal are present: broad bipartisan support for a flat fee, a Senate Republican caucus that has made regulation a fiscal priority, and a Governor who needs the revenue. What has historically prevented a deal — disagreement over the tax rate — remains the central obstacle. But the Supreme Court ruling has changed the stakes for both sides: inaction now means an enforcement action that hurts operators, hosts, and the state simultaneously.

Operators who spend July and August doing the preparation work — portfolio audits, financial modeling, PLCB compliance reviews, and legislator outreach — will be positioned to move quickly when a licensing window opens, regardless of which proposal ultimately passes. Those who wait for certainty before preparing will find themselves competing for licenses, qualified host venues, and legal counsel in a compressed window with too many other operators doing the same thing at the same time.
