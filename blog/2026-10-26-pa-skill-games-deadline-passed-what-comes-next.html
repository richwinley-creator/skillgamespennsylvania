Legislative Update

PA Skill Games Are Now Illegal: The October 13 Deadline Passed — What Happened and What Comes Next for Operators

By Skill Games Pennsylvania • October 26, 2026 • 12 min read

It is now October 26, 2026 — thirteen days after the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s 120-day stay of its June 15 ruling expired. The Pennsylvania General Assembly did not pass a regulatory bill. The Supreme Court denied an emergency extension request on October 9. At 12:01 a.m. on October 14, approximately 70,000 skill game terminals across the Commonwealth became illegal slot machines under state law, and Pennsylvania State Police enforcement is underway. For operators who have been following this fight since the June ruling, this is the moment we warned about. For operators now figuring out what to do next, this post covers exactly what happened, what the enforcement picture looks like today, and what the realistic path to legal operation looks like going forward.

What Happened on October 13: The Legislature Failed to Act

The Pennsylvania General Assembly had 120 days from June 15, 2026 to pass a regulatory framework for skill games and prevent enforcement of the Supreme Court’s ruling that those machines are unlicensed slot machines. The court granted that stay specifically to give lawmakers time to act. They did not act.

The two most viable permanent bills — Rep. Russ Diamond’s House Bills 2801 and 2802 (proposing a 20% gross terminal revenue tax restricted to liquor-licensed venues) and the bipartisan SB 1079 flat-fee package from Sens. Gene Yaw (R-23rd) and Anthony Williams (D-8th) (proposing a $500 monthly fee per terminal) — never received a committee vote in either chamber. The House Gaming Oversight Committee, chaired by Diamond himself, held no vote during the critical weeks of September and early October when a vote was still mathematically feasible. The Senate Community, Economic and Recreational Development Committee, where SB 1079 was referred, similarly took no action.

Rep. Brad Roae’s emergency extension bill — a simpler measure that would have temporarily legalized the terminals for six months to April 13, 2027, without establishing a tax structure — also failed to advance. The bill had bipartisan co-sponsorship from Rep. Manny Guzman (D-Reading), but House leadership did not move to discharge it from committee or bring it to a floor vote before October 13.

The reasons for the legislative failure are the same cross-partisan deadlocks that have blocked skill games regulation for two years running: disagreement over the tax rate (with Diamond at 20% GTR, Yaw and Williams at a flat fee, and Governor Shapiro’s administration on record at 52% GTR), disagreement over the machine cap, and disagreement over which types of locations should be eligible. The casino industry lobby, which has consistently opposed any framework that allows gambling devices to operate outside of licensed casinos, continued to pressure legislators against any bill. None of those underlying disagreements were resolved before the clock ran out.

The Supreme Court Denied the Emergency Extension Request

In the final days before the deadline, operators and industry groups filed emergency applications asking the Pennsylvania Supreme Court to extend its 120-day stay beyond October 13. The court rejected those applications on October 9, 2026 — four days before the deadline. The denial was unambiguous: the stay would expire as scheduled, and businesses must remove skill game machines from their establishments by October 13.

The court’s October 9 denial removed any remaining uncertainty about the enforcement timeline. Pennsylvania State Police confirmed in response that they would not act before October 14 — giving businesses the full duration of the stay to comply — but that enforcement would begin on October 14. Attorney General Dave Sunday, who had publicly urged businesses to remove machines ahead of the deadline in late September, added the weight of his office to the enforcement picture. The combination of PSP, the AG, and the Supreme Court’s denial of an extension left no legal avenue for machines to remain in operation after October 13.

Importantly, the court confirmed that simply powering off or unplugging an operational machine does not constitute compliance. Physical removal of machines from premises is required. An unplugged machine on site is still an unlicensed slot machine under the Gaming Act and Section 5513 of the Crimes Code.

Enforcement Is Now Active: What PA State Police and the AG Are Doing

As of October 26, Pennsylvania State Police enforcement is active and PSP has taken the step of asking the public to report businesses that are still operating skill games in defiance of the court’s ruling. That is a meaningful escalation: it signals that PSP is treating compliance as an ongoing, community-visible priority rather than a one-time sweep.

The enforcement picture is not uniform across the Commonwealth. County district attorneys retain discretion over how aggressively to pursue prosecution in their jurisdictions, and some DAs — particularly in counties with significant numbers of constituent businesses that operated these machines — may take a measured approach to individual prosecutions. However, PSP operates independently of county DAs and can execute search warrants and seize machines on its own authority. The practical risk for any operator still running machines is not limited to prosecution; it includes the immediate loss of the machines and any cash found in or around them through civil forfeiture proceedings.

The scale of the enforcement challenge is worth noting. Approximately 70,000 skill game terminals were operating in Pennsylvania as of the June ruling. Even a substantial removal effort in the weeks before October 13 cannot plausibly have cleared all of those machines. PSP and the AG are operating in an environment where many machines are gone and some remain, and their public call for community reporting is designed to identify the ones still running.

For operators who have already removed their machines: document your compliance thoroughly. Vendor pickup confirmations, photographs of empty placement areas, removal dates, and storage location records all constitute evidence of good-faith compliance that matters if any enforcement question ever arises around a former placement location.

Where the Legislation Stands: Bills Still Technically Alive Through November 30

Pennsylvania’s 2025–2026 legislative session does not end until November 30, 2026. That means the bills that failed to move before October 13 are technically still alive and could theoretically be taken up in the five weeks remaining in the session. Whether that happens depends entirely on whether legislative leadership decides to make skill games a priority before the end-of-year adjournment.

The political calculus has changed significantly since October 14. Operators whose machines have been removed have less immediate pressure on their legislators than operators whose livelihood was directly threatened by an enforcement clock. The industry’s most powerful lobbying argument — that legislators must act now or cause economic harm to their constituents — loses urgency once the harm has already occurred and machines are gone. At the same time, the revenue argument does not disappear: the estimated hundreds of millions in annual gross terminal revenue that was flowing through those 70,000 machines is now simply gone from both operators and the potential state tax base, and legislators who supported regulation will use that lost revenue as a continued argument for action.

The most likely near-term legislative scenario is not a November bill but rather pre-filing activity for the 2027 session. The bipartisan SB 1079 framework — Yaw, Williams, and their co-sponsors — has genuine support in both chambers and a policy structure (the flat monthly fee per terminal rather than a percentage of revenue) that is easier for both operators and legislators to agree on than a GTR-based tax. Diamond’s 20% GTR framework also has active sponsorship. What the November 30 session close actually means is that any bill introduced now would need to start from scratch in January 2027, since all pending legislation expires at end-of-session. Operators should expect to see refiled versions of these frameworks early in the 2027 session rather than a lame-duck push before November 30.

One important development to watch: Governor Shapiro’s administration has been consistent in its 52% GTR position throughout this legislative cycle, but that position has also been the single biggest obstacle to a deal. With enforcement now a political reality and the industry’s revenue completely gone from the state tax base, there is at least an argument that Shapiro’s team might adjust its position in 2027 negotiations. Nothing in the public record suggests that adjustment has happened yet, but it is a factor worth monitoring as 2027 prefiling begins.

The Path to 2027: What Operators Should Expect and Do Now

For Pennsylvania skill game operators navigating the post-October 13 landscape, the situation is genuinely difficult but not permanent. The political forces that drove this industry to 70,000 machines and hundreds of millions in annual revenue do not disappear because a legislative deadline was missed. The operators, location owners, VFW halls, fire companies, bars, and convenience stores that hosted these machines remain constituents, remain voters, and remain economic actors whose voice matters to legislators. The question is not whether the Pennsylvania legislature will eventually pass a skill games framework — the economic and political pressures that argue for one are too strong — but when and on what terms.

Here is what operators should do in the weeks ahead.

Maintain your vendor and location relationships. The most important asset any operator has right now is the network of placements and partnerships built over years of operation. Machines can be removed and redeployed quickly once a regulatory framework passes. Location relationships take much longer to rebuild. Communicate with your location partners, reassure them that you intend to return when the law allows, and document your agreements. A location that holds open a spot because of a strong operator relationship is a location that comes back online on day one of legal operation.

Stay current on legislation through the end of the session. November 30 is still five weeks away. While a lame-duck bill is unlikely, it is not impossible if leadership decides to act. Follow updates through the Pennsylvania General Assembly’s website and continue contacting your state representative and senator to register support for a regulatory framework in the next session. Constituent pressure from business owners is more effective when it is continuous rather than concentrated only around deadlines.

Document everything from the removal period. If your machines were removed by a vendor, obtain written confirmation of removal dates and storage locations. If you owned machines outright and transported them, maintain records of where they went. This documentation matters for any future compliance audit, civil forfeiture dispute, or insurance claim related to the transition period.

Plan for a 2027 return. The realistic timeline for legal redeployment is a 2027 bill signed in the first half of the year at the earliest, followed by whatever implementation period the regulatory framework specifies. Based on SB 1079’s proposed framework, that implementation window would likely include a licensing and registration period before machines can legally return to locations. Operators who start preparing their documentation, business structures, and location agreements now will be positioned to move quickly once a framework is signed into law.

Consult with legal counsel if you have any enforcement exposure. If your machines were still on premises after October 13, or if you have received any contact from law enforcement, consult a Pennsylvania criminal defense or gaming law attorney before taking any further action. Do not attempt to negotiate directly with PSP or remove machines during an active enforcement proceeding without legal guidance.

The Pennsylvania skill games industry has been here before in a regulatory sense — operating through years of legal uncertainty while legislators debated. The difference now is that the machines are physically gone rather than operating under uncertainty. That changes the daily economics but not the underlying political dynamics. We will continue to track every legislative development through the end of the 2026 session and into the 2027 prefiling period.
