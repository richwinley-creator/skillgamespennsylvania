Legislative Update

PA Skill Games: No Committee Vote Last Week, 8 Days Left — What October 5 Means for Every Operator

By Skill Games Pennsylvania • October 5, 2026 • 11 min read

The week of September 28 has come and gone. Our previous post identified that week as “the last moment at which legislative resolution is realistically achievable” — the window in which a committee vote had to happen if any permanent regulatory bill was going to clear both chambers and land on Governor Shapiro’s desk before October 13. No committee vote happened. Not in the House Gaming Oversight Committee on Diamond’s HB 2801 or 2802. Not in the Senate Community, Economic and Recreational Development Committee on SB 1079. Not on any bill. The Pennsylvania General Assembly spent the first week back from recess without advancing a single skill games measure out of committee, and in doing so, effectively eliminated the realistic path to permanent regulation before the enforcement deadline. Today is October 5. Eight days remain before the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s 120-day stay expires. State Police enforcement begins October 14. Every PA skill game operator needs to read this update carefully — the situation has changed in ways that require immediate action.

What the Lost Week of September 28 Actually Means

Last week’s post laid out the precise legislative sequence that any bill would need to follow to become law before October 13: a committee vote during the week of September 28, floor votes in both chambers during October 5–9, and Shapiro’s signature by October 12. That sequence was demanding but achievable if leadership in both chambers committed to it.

Leadership did not commit. The House Gaming Oversight Committee, chaired by Rep. Russ Diamond — the sponsor of HB 2801 and HB 2802 — did not schedule a hearing or a vote on any skill games bill during the week of September 28. The Senate Community, Economic and Recreational Development Committee, where SB 1079 has been referred since its introduction by Sen. Gene Yaw (R-23rd) and Sen. Anthony Williams (D-8th), also took no action. The bipartisan coalition behind SB 1079 and HB 2213, which had been the most promising vehicle for a permanent deal, was unable to force a committee calendar.

The result is arithmetically devastating for operators who were hoping for a permanent regulatory path. For both chambers to pass a bill and send it to Shapiro before October 13, everything below would need to happen between today, October 5, and October 12:

  • A committee in at least one chamber schedules and holds a vote this week — almost certainly October 6 or 7, because October 8 and 9 leave too little time for floor votes in both chambers before the weekend.
  • The full chamber holding that committee vote passes the bill on the floor by Thursday, October 8.
  • The second chamber holds its own floor vote — either concurring with the first chamber’s version or passing a version that goes back to conference — by Friday, October 10.
  • If a conference is needed, both chambers pass the final version by October 12.
  • Governor Shapiro receives the bill and signs it before midnight October 13 — a timeline he has no formal obligation to honor.

This sequence is no longer plausible for a permanent regulatory bill. The cross-partisan deadlock on the tax rate — with Diamond at 20% GTR, Yaw and Williams at a $500 flat fee, and Shapiro’s administration still on record at 52% GTR — has not been resolved. No new compromise framework has emerged. The week when resolution was still possible passed without resolution.

What this leaves is not nothing — but it is materially different from what operators were hoping for two weeks ago.

The Extension Bill: The Only Remaining Vehicle That Could Move in Time

The one legislative option that remains realistically achievable this week is the extension bill. Rep. Brad Roae (R-Crawford/Erie) circulated a co-sponsorship memo in late September proposing a six-month delay — temporarily legalizing skill game terminals without a new tax until April 13, 2027 — and Rep. Manny Guzman (D-Reading) joined as a co-sponsor, providing the bipartisan cover that such a bill would need to clear a House floor vote.

An extension bill is structurally simpler than a permanent regulatory framework. It does not require agreement on the tax rate, the machine cap, or the eligible location types that have divided the chamber for months. It requires only that a majority of members agree that the October 13 deadline should not produce mass criminal prosecutions of small business owners while a permanent deal is still being negotiated. That is a lower political bar than a permanent regulatory deal, and in a chamber where members from both parties have constituents — bar owners, fire company commanders, convenience store operators — who have been operating these machines lawfully for years, that bar may be achievable.

The challenges for the extension bill are real, however. Governor Shapiro has not publicly committed to signing any extension bill. His administration’s consistent position throughout this legislative cycle has been that skill games should be regulated and taxed, and an extension bill without a tax mechanism does not align with that position. If Shapiro vetoes an extension bill, operators are no better off than if no bill was introduced at all — they are simply waiting one more day for enforcement to begin.

The second challenge is that even a simple extension bill requires some form of committee action before a floor vote in at least one chamber. House rules generally require bills to pass through committee before reaching the floor, though leadership has procedural tools — including a rules vote to discharge a bill from committee — that can accelerate the timeline when there is political will to use them. Whether House leadership uses those tools for the extension bill this week is the most important question in Harrisburg as of October 5.

Operators should be clear-eyed about what an extension bill would and would not mean. An extension to April 13, 2027 would preserve current operations through the winter, but it would deliver six more months of regulatory uncertainty rather than a path to legal operation. Machines operating under a temporary extension would not be licensed, would not have a tax structure in place, and would still be vulnerable if the 2027 legislature fails to reach agreement on a permanent deal. An extension is a reprieve, not a resolution.

Attorney General Sunday Joins the Enforcement Warning

Pennsylvania Attorney General Dave Sunday has added the weight of his office to the enforcement picture this week, urging businesses across the state to remove skill game machines ahead of the October 13 deadline. Sunday’s public statement, issued in the final days of September, is significant for two reasons.

First, it signals that the Office of Attorney General is aligned with the Pennsylvania State Police’s enforcement posture. The PSP’s formal September 14 statement — confirming that criminal prosecution and machine seizure begin October 14 — now has explicit support from the state’s chief law enforcement officer. There is no daylight between the executive enforcement agencies on this deadline.

Second, Sunday’s statement applies statewide regardless of what individual county district attorneys decide to do. While county DAs retain discretion in their enforcement priorities, the combination of PSP and the AG’s office pursuing enforcement means that even in counties where the local DA takes a measured approach, operators are not shielded from state-level enforcement actions. PSP troopers can execute search warrants and seize machines on their own authority, without the cooperation of a local prosecutor, and those actions are backed by the Supreme Court’s June ruling.

Operators who had been monitoring their local DA’s public statements as a guide to enforcement exposure should update their calculus. The relevant enforcement question is not just what your county’s DA plans to do — it is whether PSP units operating in your region have scheduled enforcement operations for the week of October 14. PSP has confirmed it has planned for seizures. Those plans are not contingent on any county DA’s cooperation.

The Final Legislative Calendar: October 5–13

Here is the factual legislative calendar for the remaining days before the October 13 deadline and what each date means for the probability of any legislative resolution:

Monday, October 5 (today): Both chambers are in session. Today is the last day on which a committee vote could realistically happen early enough in the week to allow floor votes in both chambers before the October 13 deadline. If Diamond’s House Gaming Oversight Committee or the Senate Community, Economic and Recreational Development Committee has not announced a session for this week, the probability of a permanent bill drops to effectively zero. An emergency discharge procedure for the extension bill is still theoretically possible but requires affirmative action from House leadership.

Tuesday, October 6 – Wednesday, October 7: Floor votes would need to occur for any bill passed in committee on October 5 or 6. These two days are the final realistic window for at least one chamber to pass a bill. If a bill — whether permanent regulation or the extension — passes one chamber by Wednesday evening, there is still a path to a conference and a second chamber vote before the deadline.

Thursday, October 8 – Friday, October 9: Second-chamber floor votes would need to happen. If both chambers have passed the same version of a bill, it goes directly to Shapiro. If versions differ, a conference committee would need to convene and produce a final version this week — a procedurally compressed timeline that requires active cooperation from both chambers’ leadership.

Saturday, October 10 – Sunday, October 12: Governor Shapiro would need to receive and sign the bill. The governor’s constitutional deadline for action is ten days from receipt, but in a scenario where both chambers passed a bill this week, Shapiro would need to act over the weekend to meet the October 13 court deadline. There is no formal mechanism compelling him to sign within hours of receiving the bill, and his administration has not telegraphed whether he would sign the extension bill or the flat-fee bill under this timeline.

Monday, October 13: The Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s 120-day stay expires. If no bill is signed, skill game terminals in Pennsylvania are legally slot machines under the Gaming Act, and operating them is a criminal offense under Section 5513 of the Crimes Code.

Tuesday, October 14: State Police enforcement begins. Under the PSP’s September 14 formal statement and the AG’s October warning, criminal prosecution and seizure are authorized from this date forward for any establishment operating skill games without a signed regulatory law.

What Operators Must Do in the Next 8 Days

The absence of a committee vote last week changed the landscape from “possible but uncertain” to “unlikely unless leadership acts in the next 48 hours.” Operators who were planning around a legislative solution as the primary scenario need to rebalance their planning to treat removal as the base case with legislative resolution as the upside scenario. Here is what that means in practice.

Contact your vendor or distributor today about removal logistics. If you operate vendor-owned machines, removal must be coordinated with the machine owner. You cannot remove vendor-owned terminals yourself without authorization, and attempting to do so creates its own legal and commercial complications. Call your vendor today and confirm the removal timeline. Most major skill game distributors in Pennsylvania have been managing removal inquiries since the June ruling and can schedule removal appointments on short notice, but “short notice” means this week, not October 12. Machines that cannot be scheduled for removal before October 13 due to vendor logistics are a risk you cannot manage without that conversation happening now.

If you own your machines outright, identify a storage location and transportation. Machines removed from your premises need somewhere to go. Storing them in the back room of the same establishment is not compliance — physical removal from the premises is the standard PSP has stated. Confirm you have a storage location lined up and transportation to move the equipment before this week is over.

Track the extension bill specifically, not the permanent regulatory bills. The permanent bills — Diamond’s HB 2801/2802 and the SB 1079 flat-fee package — are not going to pass in time without a committee vote that has not yet been scheduled. If you are monitoring legislative developments as a reason to delay removal planning, the signal to watch is whether House leadership moves to discharge the extension bill for a floor vote this week. That is the only bill with a realistic legislative path in the remaining days. You can follow updates through the Pennsylvania General Assembly’s website or through local news sources covering Harrisburg.

Call your state representative and state senator one more time. The window for constituent pressure to move committee votes has effectively closed, but constituent calls about the extension bill — specifically asking your representative to support a discharge motion or floor vote on a bipartisan extension — are still relevant. Representatives who hear from business owners in their districts this week about the specific impact of enforcement on local employers are more likely to support an emergency floor procedure. Our contact page can help you identify your representatives by address.

Do not operate past October 13 under any circumstances unless a bill is signed. This is not an ambiguous legal situation. The PSP has confirmed enforcement begins October 14. The AG has publicly urged removal before October 13. The Supreme Court’s ruling has been clear since June. An operator who leaves machines running on October 14 because they believe a bill is “about to pass” is accepting criminal prosecution risk on an assumption with no current factual basis. The only protection from enforcement is a signed bill or removed machines — and as of October 5, removed machines are the only option fully within your control.

Document your removal for your legal record. Whether you remove machines this week or later, maintain documentation: vendor pickup confirmations, removal dates, storage location records, photographs of empty placement areas. If there is any enforcement action or civil forfeiture proceeding, your documentation of compliance before October 14 is your defense.

The Broader Industry Picture After October 13

Whatever happens in the next eight days, October 13 marks a structural inflection point for the Pennsylvania skill games industry rather than its endpoint. If no bill is signed and enforcement begins, it will create pressure — from operators, location owners, and the tax revenue argument — for the legislature to pass either an extension or a permanent framework early in the next session. The estimated 70,000 terminals generating hundreds of millions in annual gross revenue do not simply disappear from the political calculation when machines are seized; they become a lost revenue argument that proponents will use in January 2027 to build support for a regulatory deal.

The bipartisan SB 1079 flat-fee framework has genuine support in both chambers. Diamond’s 20% GTR proposal has an active sponsor who chairs the relevant committee. The extension bill has a bipartisan co-sponsorship. None of those political facts change on October 14. What changes is that operators without machines in their locations have less urgency to pressure legislators, and the industry’s negotiating leverage declines the longer machines stay out.

For operators who are forced to remove by October 14, the practical priority is maintaining your relationships with vendors and location partners so that re-deployment is possible quickly if and when a regulatory framework is enacted. Document your placements, maintain your location agreements, and stay engaged with your industry contacts and state representatives. The game is not over — but the next eight days are the most consequential in the history of skill games in Pennsylvania, and operators who act now are the ones best positioned for whatever comes next.
