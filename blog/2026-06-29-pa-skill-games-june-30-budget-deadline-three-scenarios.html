Pennsylvania Skill Games Face the June 30 Budget Deadline: Three Scenarios for What Happens in July

Published June 29, 2026  |  Legislative Update

Pennsylvania’s constitutional budget deadline is June 30 — today. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court issued its 4-2 ruling on June 15 that skill games are illegal slot machines, and a 120-day enforcement stay now runs until approximately October 13, 2026. Three competing legislative proposals are active: Governor Shapiro’s 52% gross revenue tax, Sen. Chris Gebhard’s 36% proposal, and the bipartisan $500-per-machine flat fee backed by Senators Yaw and Williams. Whether any of those proposals reaches the Governor’s desk today, tomorrow, or in September matters enormously to the 70,000 machines still operating across Pennsylvania. Here is what each scenario means for your operation.

Supreme Court — RULED

Skill Games Are Illegal Slot Machines

4-2 decision issued June 15, 2026. Enforcement suspended for 120 days — approximately until October 13, 2026. Machines currently operating are protected by the stay, not permanently legalized.

Budget Deadline — TODAY

June 30 Is Pennsylvania’s Constitutional Deadline

The Pennsylvania Constitution requires a balanced budget to be signed by June 30. Harrisburg has missed this deadline before, but missing it with a Supreme Court ruling in play creates unprecedented pressure to include a skill games framework.

Enforcement Window

~October 13, 2026 Exposure Date

If no regulatory framework is signed before the stay expires, law enforcement may act against unlicensed skill game terminals under the gaming statutes. Senate Republicans have called skill games a “critical piece” of the budget.

Why the Budget Deadline Matters for Skill Games

Pennsylvania’s June 30 budget deadline is constitutionally significant, but it is not the same as the skill games enforcement deadline. The two timelines are running in parallel, and understanding how they interact is essential for operators trying to plan their next steps.

The Supreme Court’s 120-day enforcement stay does not require the legislature to act by June 30 — it simply prohibits enforcement until approximately October 13, 2026, giving lawmakers time to build a framework. But the June 30 budget deadline creates a political forcing function that is arguably more powerful than the legal deadline alone. Pennsylvania faces a multi-billion-dollar budget gap. The state’s own Independent Fiscal Office has projected that a regulated skill games market could generate more than $1 billion annually for the commonwealth. That revenue is impossible to include in any budget without a regulatory framework first.

Senate Republicans have made this connection explicit, calling skill games regulation a “critical piece” of budget resolution. The Democratic-controlled House, which said it was “still reviewing” the Supreme Court’s ruling in the days immediately after June 15, has been negotiating behind closed doors. The budget deadline is the moment when those negotiations either produce a deal or confirm that skill games will be handled separately from the main budget bill — likely in a standalone vehicle over the summer.

The key distinction operators must understand: A budget deal that includes a skill games framework is the best possible outcome — it means licensing authority exists almost immediately and the path to compliance is clear. A budget that passes without skill games, or a budget impasse that drags into July, simply means the October 13 enforcement deadline remains the operative clock. Operators in either of those scenarios have more time, but no more certainty. The action items in this piece apply regardless of what happens today.

Scenario A: A Skill Games Deal Passes as Part of the Budget

Best-Case Scenario

A Regulatory Framework Is Signed Into Law in Late June or Early July

If Harrisburg reaches a comprehensive budget agreement that includes a skill games tax rate and licensing structure — whether at 52%, 36%, or the $500 flat fee — the path forward for operators becomes clear almost immediately. Here is what this scenario means in practice:

  • A licensing window opens. Any framework will include a window for existing operators to apply for licenses. The timeline is determined by the statute, but past gaming legislation in Pennsylvania has given operators 60 to 90 days to apply. Operators who are prepared — with machine inventories documented, host venue agreements reviewed, and monitoring compatibility confirmed — will be able to move within days of applications opening. Those who are not prepared will scramble.
  • Machine caps take effect. Under Shapiro’s proposal, a 40,000-machine statewide cap would require the removal of roughly 30,000 of the estimated 70,000 currently operating terminals. Under the flat-fee and Gebhard frameworks, the cap is 50,000. The operators who secure licenses first protect their positions. Location type matters: gas stations and establishments without PLCB liquor licenses are likely to be excluded under any consumer-protection overlay, including Rep. Ben Waxman’s HB 2557.
  • The 120-day stay becomes moot. If a framework is signed, the enforcement stay is superseded by the new statute. Operators who are licensed continue operating. Operators who fail to obtain a license during the application window face enforcement under the new law rather than the gambling statutes, but the outcome — losing the right to operate — is the same.
  • Tax obligations begin. Whichever rate passes, operators should model their financials immediately. At 52% gross revenue tax on a machine generating $3,000 per month, the annual tax liability is approximately $18,720 per machine. At 36%, it is approximately $12,960. At the $500 flat fee, it is $6,000 per machine annually regardless of revenue. The difference between these scenarios is the difference between a viable operation and one that needs significant restructuring.

Scenario B: Budget Passes Without a Skill Games Deal

Most Likely Near-Term Outcome

Harrisburg Passes a Budget and Handles Skill Games Separately Over the Summer

Pennsylvania has frequently passed budgets that exclude major policy issues, with those issues handled in companion legislation or standalone bills weeks or months later. If the June 30 deadline produces a budget agreement but skill games do not make it into the final package — whether due to unresolved differences on the tax rate, machine cap, or consumer protection provisions — operators are in a familiar position: the 120-day stay remains active and the October 13 enforcement date remains the operative clock.

This scenario is arguably the most likely given the complexity of the three competing proposals and the history of skill games negotiations in Harrisburg. Here is what it means:

  • The October 13 deadline becomes the primary driver. With the budget resolved, legislative attention can turn to skill games as a standalone priority. Senate Republicans, who have characterized skill games as a critical revenue source, have strong incentive to move a bill in July and August. The summer legislative calendar is shorter, but six weeks of focused negotiation could produce a floor vote before September recess.
  • Watch for committee hearings in July. If skill games are not in the budget, the Senate Finance and Appropriations Committees and the House Gaming Oversight Committee are the venues to monitor. A bill that has cleared committee in one chamber before the August recess has a realistic path to passage by mid-September — before the October 13 enforcement deadline.
  • No change in operator status. Machines currently operating remain protected by the stay. This is not a green light to expand placements — the stay protects the existing footprint, not new installations. But operators in documented, PLCB-licensed locations with monitoring-capable machines should continue operating normally while tracking legislative developments weekly.
  • Pace-O-Matic and operator associations should be your primary intelligence source. Pace-O-Matic, the major skill game software provider operating across Pennsylvania, has consistent legislative presence in Harrisburg. Their public communications and any operator association updates will be the fastest way to track bill movement between now and October.

Scenario C: Budget Impasse With Skill Games Unresolved

Worst-Case Near-Term Scenario

No Budget Deal and Skill Games Left in Legislative Limbo Into July

Pennsylvania has experienced budget impasses that lasted weeks or months: the 2019 impasse stretched into November. A prolonged budget fight would absorb most of the legislative bandwidth in Harrisburg and push skill games negotiations to the back of the queue. This is the scenario operators least want to see, and it is also the scenario that most compresses the effective window for a skill games deal before October 13.

  • A budget impasse does not stop the October 13 clock. The Supreme Court’s 120-day stay runs regardless of what happens in the budget. If a budget impasse drags into August, the remaining window for a skill games bill narrows from roughly three months to six weeks or fewer.
  • Pressure on lawmakers intensifies rapidly. A budget impasse combined with an October 13 enforcement deadline puts legislators in an uncomfortable position: they must simultaneously resolve the budget and pass skill games legislation while the enforcement clock ticks. That pressure can accelerate resolution or cause it to collapse entirely if the politics of skill games become a bargaining chip in budget negotiations.
  • Prepare for the worst-case: no framework before October 13. If both a budget impasse and a skill games legislative failure materialize simultaneously — a low-probability but not impossible outcome — operators face enforcement exposure when the stay expires. In that environment, machines at non-PLCB-licensed locations face the highest risk. Gas stations and convenience stores without liquor licenses would be enforcement priorities under any reasonable interpretation of the gaming statutes. Begin identifying alternative PLCB-licensed host venues now, not in September.
  • Consult legal counsel. A budget impasse scenario is the one where having a Pennsylvania gaming attorney on call matters most. The legal landscape can shift quickly and the difference between a protected operation and an exposed one can come down to location type, machine documentation, and the specific enforcement policy a district attorney or the Attorney General chooses to apply.

The Three Active Legislative Proposals — Where Each Stands

Regardless of which scenario plays out today, these are the three vehicles operators need to track. Any one of them could become law between now and October 13.

Proposal Tax / Fee Machine Cap Key Supporters Operator Impact
Gov. Shapiro Budget Proposal 52% of gross revenue 40,000 statewide; 5 per establishment Shapiro administration; casino-aligned legislators Heaviest tax burden; forces removal of ~30,000 machines; generates $765.9M in year one, $2B+ at maturity
Sen. Gebhard (R., Lebanon) Proposal 36% of gross revenue 50,000; restricted to PLCB-licensed venues Senate Republicans; aligned with PGCB oversight model Manageable rate for high-volume locations; gas station placements eliminated; PLCB-licensed bars and restaurants protected
SB 1079 — Yaw / Williams / Burgos Flat Fee $500 per machine per month 50,000 statewide; 5 per establishment Bipartisan; industry-backed; Pace-O-Matic aligned; broadest co-sponsor support Lowest effective rate for high-revenue machines; predictable fixed cost; most operator-friendly structure

The bipartisan flat-fee approach has consistently had the broadest co-sponsor support in both chambers. The revenue case for the flat fee is also the easiest to make to House Democrats who are concerned about operator profitability driving compliance: at $6,000 per machine annually, operators can remain viable, which means machines stay licensed and generating revenue for the state rather than going dark or operating underground. Senator Gene Yaw has been the primary Senate champion of the lower-rate approach; Rep. Danilo Burgos has carried the House companion.

Sen. Gebhard’s 36% proposal sits between the two extremes and has the advantage of being the Senate Republican caucus’s preferred structured-rate option. If negotiations between Shapiro and the Senate produce a compromise, 36% is the most likely landing zone for a percentage-based rate. Operators running high-margin, high-volume locations — busy bars and restaurants with consistent weeknight and weekend traffic — can model this rate as survivable. Operators whose footprint includes lower-traffic venues need to run their numbers carefully under 36%.

Shapiro’s 52% proposal remains the most consequential for industry structure. At that rate, the effective margin compression on most machines would force significant consolidation — fewer operators, fewer host locations, and a likely dramatic reduction in the number of terminals from the current estimated 70,000 to something closer to the 40,000-machine cap. The Shapiro administration’s $765.9 million first-year revenue projection is based on this rate and this cap. The Independent Fiscal Office has confirmed that regulated skill games could generate more than $1 billion annually, though the specific rate used in that projection aligns more closely with a blended scenario than the Shapiro maximum.

What Operators Must Do Before July 31

The budget deadline creates a natural break point: whatever happens today, July 1 begins a compressed period that operators need to use productively. The following actions apply under all three scenarios and should be completed in the next 30 days regardless of legislative outcome.

Complete a Full Machine and Location Inventory

  • Document every machine by street address, venue name, venue type, and whether the host holds a PLCB liquor license. This data is the foundation of any licensing application and the basis for any compliance conversation with a host venue.
  • Flag every gas station and unlicensed convenience store placement. Under HB 2557 and the Gebhard framework, these locations will not qualify for licensing. Begin the conversation with those hosts now about the regulatory landscape, and start identifying PLCB-licensed alternatives. Finding a qualified replacement location takes four to eight weeks when done without pressure; it takes a month in a crisis.
  • Record each machine’s make, model, and serial number. Licensing applications will require this at a minimum. Banilla Games, JVL Entertainment, and Primero are the three primary manufacturers with distribution in Pennsylvania; confirm with your distributor which firmware versions are being positioned as compliant under the PGCB monitoring framework.

Model Your Financials Under Each Rate Scenario

  • Run the numbers for every machine in your portfolio at all three rate structures: 52%, 36%, and $500/month flat fee. You need to know, before a bill passes, which machines are viable under which scenario and which are not. Machines that are not viable at 52% but are viable at 36% represent a portfolio risk if Shapiro wins the rate negotiation.
  • If your revenue-share agreement with host venues is 50/50, consider what renegotiation looks like under each scenario. A location where the skill games revenue barely covers the host’s share at 36% gross tax is a location you may need to exit before it becomes a licensing liability. Having those conversations in July, from a position of preparation, is very different from having them in September under deadline pressure.
  • Factor in the machine cap risk. If the final cap is 40,000 and licenses are issued on a first-come basis, late applicants may find their desired locations already licensed by a competitor. Speed of application matters as much as eligibility when cap competition is part of the framework.

Engage Your State Legislators Directly

  • The skill games debate is not decided yet, and legislator contacts from constituents — particularly small business owners and their employees — still move votes. If your machines are in bars, restaurants, or convenience stores in a particular district, the owner of that location calling their State Representative or State Senator is more effective than any trade association communication.
  • The argument that resonates most in Harrisburg right now is a fiscal one: regulated skill games at a reasonable rate generate more than $1 billion annually for Pennsylvania, while an unworkable rate produces machine shutdowns and zero revenue. Make that argument clearly and with local business context.
  • The Pennsylvania Skill Games Association and Pace-O-Matic are coordinating legislative outreach. Connect with them if you have not already. They have real-time intelligence on bill movement and can direct operator engagement to the legislators where it will have the most impact.

Understand the Enforcement Landscape if No Deal Is Reached

  • The 120-day stay protects current operators through approximately October 13. It does not protect new placements made during the stay period, and it does not protect operators who expand into location types that are clearly going to be excluded from any regulatory framework.
  • If October 13 arrives with no framework in place, the exposure is not uniform across operator types. Machines at PLCB-licensed bars and restaurants, operated by documented business entities with clean records, are lower enforcement priorities than machines at unlicensed locations operated by parties with no paper trail.
  • Have a Pennsylvania attorney familiar with gaming law on retainer or at minimum identified and available. If the October 13 deadline arrives with no deal, the period from October 14 onward requires real-time legal guidance that most operators are not positioned to get on short notice.

The Political Dynamics Heading Into July

The budget deadline is a political pressure release valve, but it is not the end of the skill games negotiation. Understanding the political dynamics that will shape July and August helps operators anticipate where the legislative process goes next.

Senate Republicans have the most to gain from a skill games deal that passes before the budget: they can claim fiscal responsibility (generating state revenue without raising taxes), economic pragmatism (protecting small businesses), and bipartisan leadership on a complex issue. Their preferred structure — Gebhard’s 36% or the flat-fee approach — is meaningfully different from Shapiro’s 52% position, but not so far apart that a compromise is impossible. The most likely negotiated outcome is a rate in the 30-40% range or a flat fee with a per-machine cap slightly above Shapiro’s $500 figure.

House Democrats control the lower chamber and have the leverage to insist on consumer protection provisions as a condition of any vote. Rep. Waxman’s HB 2557 — with its $250 daily loss cap, age-21 identity verification, and gas station ban — represents the floor of what the Democratic caucus will accept as a consumer protection overlay. Operators who have not yet read HB 2557 should do so before July 1. Its provisions, or something close to them, will almost certainly be in any final package.

Governor Shapiro’s 52% proposal has been consistent across two budget cycles, but the Governor has also signaled that he primarily wants a framework — not necessarily a specific rate. The 52% number is a negotiating ceiling, not a fixed demand. A compromise that delivers the licensing and monitoring structure the administration wants at a lower rate is a deal the Governor could sign. Operators should not treat the 52% figure as inevitable; treat it as the starting point for a negotiation that is still in progress.

The Bottom Line for Pennsylvania Skill Game Operators

Today is the budget deadline. Tomorrow begins July. The Supreme Court’s 120-day window runs until approximately October 13. Whatever happens with the budget, operators who spend July preparing — documenting their portfolios, modeling their financials, engaging their legislators, and identifying compliant host locations — will be in the strongest possible position when a licensing framework opens.

The operators who survive this period and come out licensed and compliant will be the ones who treated the budget deadline not as a resolution, but as a starting gun for the work that needs to happen between now and October. The legislative process in Harrisburg is uncertain. Your preparation does not have to be.
