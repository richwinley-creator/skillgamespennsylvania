Legislative Update

PA House Returns, Diamond Files HB 2801-2802: The Final 15-Day Sprint to October 13

By Skill Games Pennsylvania • September 28, 2026 • 12 min read

Last week’s post identified September 28 as “the definitive signal” for Pennsylvania’s skill games future: either committee votes move forward on the week the House returns, or the probability of pre-deadline legislation collapses to near zero. Today, that signal begins. The Pennsylvania House of Representatives reconvened this morning after a week-long dark period, opening the last realistic window before the October 13 enforcement deadline. Into that window stepped Rep. Russ Diamond, the Republican chairman of the House Gaming Oversight Committee, with two new bills — HB 2801 and HB 2802 — proposing a 20% gross terminal revenue tax and structural guardrails that differ meaningfully from every other framework on the table. With 15 days remaining, here is what every PA skill game operator needs to know about where things stand on September 28.

What Diamond’s HB 2801 and HB 2802 Actually Propose

Representative Russ Diamond, a Lebanon County Republican who chairs the House Gaming Oversight Committee, introduced House Bills 2801 and 2802 as companion legislation in the days leading up to the House’s September 28 return. Diamond has been one of the most consistent voices in Harrisburg calling for a regulatory framework that keeps skill games viable for small business operators, and his new proposals reflect that orientation — while also incorporating structural restrictions that will directly affect who can operate machines and how many.

The core of Diamond’s framework is a 20% gross terminal revenue (GTR) tax. That rate is the lowest of the three active tax frameworks in the current legislative debate, sitting well below Governor Shapiro’s 52% GTR proposal and below the flat-fee structure in SB 1079 that, depending on machine revenue, can translate to an effective rate between 12% and 25%. For the average skill game terminal generating $500–$700 in gross terminal revenue per month, Diamond’s 20% rate translates to a state obligation of $100–$140 monthly per machine — a figure that most operators who have modeled their locations call workable.

But the 20% rate comes with significant structural conditions. Diamond’s bills would:

  • Limit placements to five machines per qualifying establishment. The five-machine cap applies across the categories of locations Diamond’s bill permits, which means high-volume operators running eight or ten machines at a single location would need to reduce their floor count to comply.
  • Restrict eligible establishments to liquor-licensed venues and qualified truck stops. This is the provision with the largest structural impact. A significant share of the approximately 70,000 skill game terminals currently operating in Pennsylvania sit inside convenience stores, car washes, laundromats, and other retail locations that do not hold a liquor license. Diamond’s framework would exclude those locations entirely, regardless of whether they have operated lawfully for years under the previous legal understanding.
  • Direct a portion of tax revenue toward property tax relief for Pennsylvania homeowners. Diamond’s package is designed as part of a broader gaming modernization effort that would use skill game revenue to reduce property taxes for an estimated 2.87 million Pennsylvania homeowners. This revenue-use provision is designed to build bipartisan support by framing the tax as relief rather than new spending.

The liquor license restriction is the most commercially significant element of Diamond’s proposal and the one operators in the convenience and fuel retail sector are watching most closely. If Diamond’s framework becomes law, a convenience store or truck stop without a liquor license would have no legal path to continue operating skill games — regardless of how long those machines have been in place. For the thousands of operators in that category, Diamond’s bills would be a regulatory exit from the industry, not a regulatory home.

Diamond was explicit about the urgency driving his introduction: with the October 13 deadline weeks away and no committee vote yet taken on any bill, he framed HB 2801 and HB 2802 as a path that can attract committee support quickly because the 20% rate and the property tax connection give members a politically defensible yes vote. Whether that logic translates to committee scheduling in the next week is the question the entire industry is watching.

The Four Active Frameworks: Where Each Stands Today

Pennsylvania skill game operators are now navigating four active legislative frameworks simultaneously. Understanding the differences — and the political feasibility of each — is essential as the final window narrows.

SB 1079 / HB 2213 — $500 flat fee per terminal per month. Sponsored in the Senate by Sen. Gene Yaw (R-23rd) and Sen. Anthony Williams (D-8th), with a House companion from Rep. Danilo Burgos (D-197th) and Rep. Jonathan Fritz (R-139th), this is the most bipartisan bill currently in play. The $500 flat fee generates roughly $300 million annually with a 50,000-machine statewide cap. For operators running machines in high-revenue locations, the flat fee is significantly cheaper than any percentage-based rate at typical machine earnings. For operators in low-revenue locations, the flat fee cuts deeper proportionally. The bill has not yet received a committee vote in either chamber, but its bipartisan co-sponsorship makes it the most likely vehicle to clear committee if leaders decide to move this week.

HB 2801 / HB 2802 — Diamond’s 20% GTR package. As described above: the lowest percentage rate of any active proposal, but paired with a liquor-license restriction that would eliminate a large segment of current operators. Diamond chairs the House Gaming Oversight Committee, which means he has scheduling power that most bill sponsors do not — he can call a committee vote on his own bills. Whether he does so in the week of September 28 is the most important near-term legislative development to watch.

Governor Shapiro’s 52% GTR position. Shapiro has not introduced a standalone bill, but his administration has consistently and publicly called for a 52% gross terminal revenue tax. That rate would generate between $500 million and $800 million annually, depending on final machine counts and revenue. Operators who have stress-tested this rate against their location economics have broadly concluded that it destroys the economics of community venue placements — bars, fire halls, VFWs, neighborhood restaurants. Shapiro has not indicated whether he would sign either the flat-fee bill or Diamond’s package; any public statement from his office about those specific bills this week would be the single most important development of the final sprint.

Rep. Roae’s extension bill — six months of temporary legalization. As covered in last week’s post, Rep. Brad Roae (R-Crawford/Erie) circulated a co-sponsorship memo for a bill that would temporarily legalize machines without a tax until April 13, 2027, giving the legislature six more months to negotiate a permanent framework. Roae has not yet introduced the formal bill with a number. The extension bill’s appeal is that it removes the October 13 cliff without requiring a tax rate compromise that neither side has been willing to make. Its risk is that it produces six more months of regulatory limbo with no revenue collected, which makes it harder — not easier — to get Shapiro’s signature.

State Police: Seizure Operations Begin October 14

The Pennsylvania State Police have been unambiguous and consistent in their enforcement posture, and nothing from the last week has softened it. PSP’s formal statement, issued September 14, remains operative: as of October 14, 2026, all skill game terminals remaining on any premises — licensed or unlicensed — are subject to immediate seizure and the operator faces criminal prosecution.

Several aspects of the enforcement picture deserve operator attention as of September 28:

Seizure logistics are already planned. PSP has been preparing removal logistics since the June 15 Supreme Court ruling. By the time of the September 14 formal warning, enforcement plans were in place at the regional level. The PSP is not going to improvise a seizure operation on October 14; it is executing a plan that has been in development for months.

Criminal prosecution exposure is real, not theoretical. The PSP statement explicitly says operators are “subject to criminal prosecution.” The Crimes Code definition of illegal slot machine — which the Supreme Court applied to skill games in its June ruling — carries criminal penalties. Some county district attorneys have signaled a measured approach; others, including Berks County DA John Adams, have been more aggressive in their public statements, suggesting they are prepared to execute search warrants and seize machines at volume. Operators should not assume that their county’s DA will take a passive stance in the absence of specific public confirmation of that posture.

Powering off or disconnecting machines does not constitute compliance. This has been consistent in every PSP communication. Physical removal of the terminals from the premises is the only recognized form of compliance. Operators who unplug machines and leave them in storage on the same premises have not complied under the terms of the PSP enforcement framework.

Enforcement will not wait for the legislature. If no bill is signed by October 13, the PSP enforcement plan does not pause for legislative developments. An operator who leaves machines in place on October 14 betting that a bill is “close” to passing is operating outside the law from the moment the deadline passes.

The 15-Day Calendar: What Must Happen for Any Bill to Pass

Even with the House back in session as of today, the legislative math for passing any bill before October 13 is demanding. Here is the sequence that would need to occur:

Week of September 28 — October 4 (this week): A committee vote must happen. Without a committee vote in at least one chamber during this week, there are effectively no remaining legislative days to pass a bill through both chambers and get a signature before October 13. The committees with jurisdiction — the Senate Community, Economic and Recreational Development Committee for SB 1079, and the House Gaming Oversight Committee for HB 2213, HB 2801, or Roae’s extension bill once it receives a number — need to act. Rep. Diamond’s chairmanship of the House Gaming Oversight Committee gives him direct scheduling authority over his own bills. Whether he uses that authority this week is the clearest single indicator of whether the permanent regulatory path is still alive.

Week of October 5–9 (final full week before deadline): Both chambers would need floor votes. The Senate would need to pass a bill and the House would need to concur, or both chambers would need independent floor votes on their respective bill versions followed by a conference if the versions differ. In a closely divided legislature with cross-partisan disagreements on the tax rate, this is a fast timeline but not an impossible one if leadership commits to it.

By October 12: Governor Shapiro would need to receive and sign the bill. Shapiro has 10 days to act on legislation, but in a deadline scenario, he would need to act within hours or days of receiving the bill to make the October 13 date. There is no formal timeline requiring him to sign quickly, which means even a bill that passes both chambers by October 10 faces a governor’s desk uncertainty if Shapiro has not publicly committed to signing it.

This calendar is achievable but it requires leadership in both chambers to prioritize the votes, and it requires at least a tacit signal from Shapiro that he will not veto the bill once it arrives. Neither of those conditions has been met as of September 28. The next 72 hours — the first three days of the legislature’s return — will tell operators more about the probability of a pre-deadline deal than everything that happened in the three weeks prior.

What Diamond’s Bills Mean for Different Operator Categories

Because Diamond’s HB 2801 and HB 2802 introduce a new framework with a liquor-license restriction that does not appear in SB 1079 or HB 2213, different operators face meaningfully different outcomes depending on which bill becomes law. Here is what each category needs to understand:

Bars, taverns, and liquor-licensed restaurants. Diamond’s framework is designed for you. At 20% GTR and up to five machines, a bar generating $600 per machine per month in gross terminal revenue pays $120 per machine monthly to the state — a cost structure that preserves viability at most mid-volume locations. The five-machine cap may require some operators to remove terminals, but the economics of the remaining machines are protected at the 20% rate.

Qualified truck stops. Diamond’s bills explicitly include qualified truck stops as eligible locations, which means operators at large fuel-retail locations with significant truck traffic should be able to continue under his framework. The definition of “qualified” will matter at the regulatory implementation level, but the category is preserved.

Convenience stores without a liquor license. Diamond’s framework offers no path forward. If HB 2801 and 2802 pass, convenience store operators who do not hold a liquor license will need to remove their machines regardless of the tax rate. For these operators, the only frameworks that preserve their operations are SB 1079/HB 2213 (which does not contain a location restriction) or Roae’s extension bill (which preserves the current status quo temporarily). The relative success of SB 1079 vs. Diamond’s package in the coming week will determine which of those paths survives.

Fire companies, VFWs, and social clubs. These venues have been among the most vocal advocates for skill game preservation in Pennsylvania, and many of them hold liquor licenses. Diamond’s framework would likely cover them, though the five-machine cap would apply. SB 1079’s flat fee may be more advantageous for these locations depending on their machine revenue levels.

Game room operators. Standalone game room businesses — those operating primarily or exclusively as skill game venues — face a more uncertain picture under Diamond’s proposal. Whether a dedicated game room qualifies as a liquor-licensed establishment or a qualified truck stop depends on its specific license structure. Operators in this category should consult with their distributor and legal counsel about how their specific location type is classified under each of the active legislative proposals.

What Operators Must Do Before October 13

The week of September 28 is not a week for waiting. It is the last week in which legislative action is realistic, and regardless of what happens in Harrisburg, every operator needs to be managing two scenarios simultaneously: a legislative solution that allows continued operation, and a removal scenario that prevents criminal prosecution and seizure.

Know which bill framework applies to your location. If Diamond’s 20% GTR package advances, do you hold a liquor license? If not, SB 1079’s flat fee is the framework that keeps your operation legal, and you should be tracking its committee progress specifically. If you are in a liquor-licensed bar or restaurant, Diamond’s bills and SB 1079 both offer paths forward, but at different economics. Understanding which framework is better for your location is not abstract at this point — it directly determines what you should be advocating to your representatives this week.

Contact your state representative and state senator before the week is out. The committee votes that will determine the legislative outcome need to happen this week. Representatives who hear from operators in their districts about which bill they support and why are more likely to push committee chairs to schedule a vote. A call to your representative’s office asking specifically whether the House Gaming Oversight Committee will schedule HB 2801 or HB 2213 for a vote this week takes five minutes and matters. Our contact page has tools for identifying your representatives by address.

Have your removal plan ready to execute. Even if you believe legislation will pass, you need a removal plan that can be executed before October 14 if no bill is signed. That means you have spoken to your vendor or distributor, confirmed the removal logistics, and identified the timeline required to physically move your terminals. For vendor-owned machines, removal is coordinated with the machine owner; you cannot remove them yourself without authorization. For operator-owned machines, you need a location for the terminals and a logistics plan. If you have not had this conversation with your vendor, have it today.

Do not interpret silence from Harrisburg as safety. The absence of bad news is not good news in a 15-day window. If the week of September 28 passes without a committee vote on any of the active bills, no announcement from the legislature signals that the clock is still running — it means enforcement is 15 days away and no protection exists. The default outcome, absent legislative action, is enforcement. Plan accordingly.

The Pennsylvania skill games industry has been navigating legislative uncertainty for years, and has survived budget deadlines, court decisions, and regulatory proposals from multiple directions. The October 13 deadline is different: it has a specific enforcement mechanism, a specific date, and a state police force that has explicitly confirmed its enforcement plans. The week of September 28 is the last moment at which legislative resolution is realistically achievable. Whether Harrisburg uses that moment — or whether operators find themselves executing removal plans on October 10 — will become clear within days.
